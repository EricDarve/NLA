QR Iteration#

The derivations from orthogonal iteration lead to the celebrated QR iteration method, a fundamental algorithm for computing the Schur decomposition and eigenvalues of a matrix.

In the QR iteration, we focus on computing the sequence of matrices:

\[ T_k = Q_k^H A Q_k \]

where \(Q_k\) is the sequence of unitary matrices produced by orthogonal iteration.

Let’s consider the relationship between \(T_k\) and the next matrix in the sequence, \(T_{k+1}\):

\[ T_{k+1} = Q_{k+1}^H A Q_{k+1} \]

Our goal is to find a way to compute \(T_{k+1}\) directly from \(T_k\) without needing to compute \(A\) or the full \(Q_k\) matrices.

For the method of QR iteration, the matrix \(Q_k\) is assumed to be square (i.e., we are computing all eigenvalues of a square matrix \(A\)).

Derivation from Orthogonal Iteration#

The derivation proceeds in two main steps.

Step 1: Relate \(T_k\) to a QR Decomposition#

From the definition of orthogonal iteration, we have the relation \(A Q_k = Q_{k+1} R_{k+1}\). We can substitute this into the definition of \(T_k\):

\[ T_k = Q_k^H (A Q_k) = Q_k^H (Q_{k+1} R_{k+1}) \]

Let’s define a new unitary matrix \(U_{k+1}\) as:

\[ U_{k+1} \overset{def}{=} Q_k^H Q_{k+1} \]

The relation \(Q_{k+1}=Q_kU_{k+1}\) expresses the change of basis between consecutive iterations. It includes any sign or phase changes in the columns.

Substituting this definition, we get:

\[ T_k = U_{k+1} R_{k+1} \]

This equation is simply the QR decomposition of the matrix \(T_k\). This is a key insight: \(U_{k+1}\) and \(R_{k+1}\) can be found by performing a QR decomposition on \(T_k\), without any knowledge of \(A\), \(Q_k\), or \(Q_{k+1}\).

Step 2: Relate \(T_{k+1}\) to \(T_k\)#

Now let’s find an expression for \(T_{k+1}\) in terms of \(T_k\). We start with the definition \(T_k = Q_k^H A Q_k\), which (assuming \(Q_k\) is square) implies \(A = Q_k T_k Q_k^H\).

Substitute this expression for \(A\) into the definition of \(T_{k+1}\):

\[ T_{k+1} = Q_{k+1}^H A Q_{k+1} = Q_{k+1}^H (Q_k T_k Q_k^H) Q_{k+1} \]

By grouping the terms, we get:

\[ T_{k+1} = (Q_{k+1}^H Q_k) T_k (Q_k^H Q_{k+1}) \]

Recall our definition \(U_{k+1} = Q_k^H Q_{k+1}\). This means \(U_{k+1}^H = (Q_k^H Q_{k+1})^H = Q_{k+1}^H Q_k\). Substituting these into the equation for \(T_{k+1}\) gives:

\[ T_{k+1} = U_{k+1}^H T_k U_{k+1} \]

This shows that \(T_k\) and \(T_{k+1}\) are unitarily similar, which confirms they have the same eigenvalues.

Now, we combine the results from Step 1 and Step 2.

From Step 1: \(T_k = U_{k+1} R_{k+1}\).

Left-multiplying by \(U_{k+1}^H\), we get

\[U_{k+1}^H T_k = U_{k+1}^H (U_{k+1} R_{k+1}) = R_{k+1}.\]

Now substitute this into our equation for \(T_{k+1}\):

\[ T_{k+1} = (U_{k+1}^H T_k) U_{k+1} = R_{k+1} U_{k+1} \]

This provides the final, simple relationship:

  1. \(T_k = U_{k+1} R_{k+1}\)

  2. \(T_{k+1} = R_{k+1} U_{k+1}\)

This is the basis for the method of QR iteration.

Summary of the Key Steps#

The derivation shows that we can generate the sequence \(T_{k+1}\) from \(T_k\) using two simple steps:

  1. Step 1: Compute the QR decomposition of \(T_k\): \(T_k = U_{k+1} R_{k+1}\)

  2. Step 2: Compute \(T_{k+1}\) by multiplying the factors in reverse order: \(T_{k+1} = R_{k+1} U_{k+1}\)

We just switch the order of the terms! We can compute the entire sequence of \(T_k\) matrices starting from \(T_0 = A\) without ever needing to form the \(Q_k\) matrices from the orthogonal iteration.

With positive diagonal entries in each QR factor \(R_k\), this QR iteration produces the same sequence of \(R_k\) matrices as orthogonal iteration.

From \(U_{k+1}=Q_k^HQ_{k+1}\), the product \(U_1\cdots U_k\) equals \(Q_k\) from orthogonal iteration when \(Q_0=I\). Under the strict-gap and starting-space conditions established there, its columns approach the ordered Schur directions. Signs or complex phases may continue to change.

Recall the QR identity for orthogonal iteration:

\[ A^k = Q_k \, R_k R_{k-1}\cdots R_1. \]

Using the new sequence of \(U_k\) matrices from the QR iteration, we can write

\[ A^k = (U_1 U_2 \cdots U_k) (R_k R_{k-1}\cdots R_1). \]

We restate this important result as a formal theorem.

Theorem 27 (Relationship Between \(A^k\) and the QR Iteration Factors)

Let \(A\) be a square matrix. Let the sequences of matrices \(\{T_k\}\), \(\{U_k\}\), and \(\{R_k\}\) be generated by QR iteration, defined as:

  1. Initialize \(T_0 = A\).

  2. For \(k=0, 1, 2, \dots\):

    • Compute the QR decomposition: \(T_k = U_{k+1} R_{k+1}\)

    • Compute the next iterate: \(T_{k+1} = R_{k+1} U_{k+1}\)

where \(U_{k+1}\) is a unitary matrix and \(R_{k+1}\) is an upper triangular matrix.

Use the same accumulated triangular factor \(\widetilde R_k=R_k\cdots R_1\) as in orthogonal iteration. Define the product matrices:

  • \(\widetilde Q_k = U_1 U_2 \cdots U_k\)

  • \(\widetilde R_k = R_k R_{k-1} \cdots R_1\)

Then for any integer \(k \ge 1\), the \(k\)-th power of the matrix \(A\) is given by:

\[ A^k = \widetilde Q_k \widetilde R_k \]

The proof is provided at the end of this section.

Thus \(\widetilde Q_k=Q_k\) when orthogonal iteration starts from \(Q_0=I\).

Convergence Rate#

The subspace convergence result, Theorem 26, explains how quickly the leading column spaces of \(Q_k\) approach invariant subspaces. For QR iteration, we need to translate this geometric error into an error in \(T_k=Q_k^HAQ_k\). The connection is the block below the diagonal: as a leading column space becomes invariant, this block approaches zero.

From Subspace Error to Entries Below the Diagonal#

Fix \(1\le i<n\). Let \(Q_{k,i}\) contain the first \(i\) columns of \(Q_k\), and let \(Q_{k,\perp}\) contain its remaining columns. Then

\[\begin{split} T_k= \begin{pmatrix} Q_{k,i}^HAQ_{k,i} & Q_{k,i}^HAQ_{k,\perp}\\ \underbrace{Q_{k,\perp}^HAQ_{k,i}}_{B_{k,i}} & Q_{k,\perp}^HAQ_{k,\perp} \end{pmatrix}. \end{split}\]

The lower-left block \(B_{k,i}=(T_k)_{i+1:n,\,1:i}\) measures the component of \(AQ_{k,i}\) outside the first \(i\) columns. It is zero exactly when their span is invariant. Thus convergence of this subspace should make this entire block small, even if the columns still rotate within the subspace.

Write the orthogonal projectors onto the current and exact leading spaces as

\[ \Pi_{k,i}=Q_{k,i}Q_{k,i}^H, \qquad \Pi_{\star,i}=Q_{\star,i}Q_{\star,i}^H. \]

As established for orthogonal iteration, the subspace error can be written as

\[ d_{k,i}=\|\Pi_{k,i}-\Pi_{\star,i}\|_2 =\|(I-\Pi_{\star,i})Q_{k,i}\|_2. \]

The extra index \(i\) records which leading subspace is being measured. Since \(Q_k\) is unitary,

\[ \|B_{k,i}\|_2 =\|(I-\Pi_{k,i})A\Pi_{k,i}\|_2. \]

The exact Schur space is invariant, so \((I-\Pi_{\star,i})A\Pi_{\star,i}=0\). Subtract this zero term and regroup:

\[\begin{split} \begin{aligned} (I-\Pi_{k,i})A\Pi_{k,i} &=(I-\Pi_{k,i})A(\Pi_{k,i}-\Pi_{\star,i})\\ &\quad +(\Pi_{\star,i}-\Pi_{k,i})A\Pi_{\star,i}. \end{aligned} \end{split}\]

Taking norms gives the desired connection:

\[ \boxed{\|B_{k,i}\|_2\le 2\|A\|_2\,d_{k,i}.} \]

Theorem 28 (Convergence of a Lower-Left Block in QR Iteration)

Suppose \(A=X\Lambda X^{-1}\) is nonsingular, with eigenvalues ordered by decreasing magnitude. Fix \(1\le i<n\) and assume

\[ |\lambda_i|>|\lambda_{i+1}|. \]

Assume also that the leading \(i\times i\) submatrix of \(X^{-1}\) is invertible. This is the starting-space condition for the first \(i\) columns of \(Q_0=I\).

Then unshifted QR iteration starting from \(T_0=A\) satisfies, in exact arithmetic and for \(k\ge1\),

\[\begin{split} \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \|(T_k)_{i+1:n,\,1:i}\|_2 &\le 2\|A\|_2K_i\rho_i^k,\\ \rho_i&=\left|\frac{\lambda_{i+1}}{\lambda_i}\right|<1. \end{aligned} } \end{split}\]

Here \(K_i\) is the constant in Theorem 26 applied to the first \(i\) starting columns.

Proof. The subspace theorem gives \(d_{k,i}\le K_i\rho_i^k\). Substituting this into the preceding estimate \(\|B_{k,i}\|_2\le2\|A\|_2d_{k,i}\) proves the result.

This bound needs only the gap at position \(i\) and the corresponding starting-space condition. For example, with \(i=n-1\), it bounds all entries to the left of the bottom-right diagonal entry. A small ratio \(|\lambda_n/\lambda_{n-1}|\) makes the last eigenvalue separate quickly.

Error Bound for the Schur Approximation#

To make every block below the diagonal converge, apply the preceding argument to every leading subspace.

Theorem 29 (Convergence Rate of Unshifted QR Iteration)

Suppose \(n\ge2\), \(A=X\Lambda X^{-1}\), and

\[ |\lambda_1|>\cdots>|\lambda_n|>0. \]

For each \(1\le i<n\), assume the first \(i\) rows of \(X^{-1}Q_{0,i}\) form an invertible \(i\times i\) matrix, where \(Q_{0,i}\) contains the first \(i\) columns of \(I\). Let \(A=Q_\star TQ_\star^H\) be the Schur decomposition in this eigenvalue order, and define

\[ \rho=\max_{1\le i<n} \left|\frac{\lambda_{i+1}}{\lambda_i}\right|<1. \]

In exact arithmetic, there are diagonal unitary phase matrices \(\Phi_k\) and a constant \(C\), independent of \(k\), such that, for \(k\ge1\),

\[ \boxed{\|\Phi_kT_k\Phi_k^H-T\|_2\le C\rho^k.} \]

In particular, without any phase adjustment,

\[\begin{split} \begin{aligned} |(T_k)_{jj}-\lambda_j|&\le C\rho^k,\\ |(T_k)_{j\ell}|&\le C\rho^k\qquad(j>\ell). \end{aligned} \end{split}\]

The starting-space condition is the same one used for orthogonal iteration, specialized to \(Q_0=I\). The constant depends on the eigenvector basis and the starting coordinates and can be large for a nonnormal matrix.

Proof. The subspace theorem gives \(d_{k,i}\le K_i\rho_i^k\le K_i\rho^k\). As on the orthogonal-iteration page, subtracting consecutive projectors isolates column \(i\):

\[ q_{k,i}q_{k,i}^H=\Pi_{k,i}-\Pi_{k,i-1}. \]

Set \(d_{k,0}=d_{k,n}=0\), since the zero space and the full space are already exact. The triangle inequality gives

\[ \|q_{k,i}q_{k,i}^H-q_{\star,i}q_{\star,i}^H\|_2 \le d_{k,i}+d_{k,i-1}. \]

Choose \(\phi_{k,i}\) with \(|\phi_{k,i}|=1\) to align \(q_{k,i}\) with \(q_{\star,i}\). For two unit vectors, this phase choice gives

\[\begin{split} \begin{aligned} \|q_{k,i}\overline{\phi_{k,i}}-q_{\star,i}\|_2^2 &=2(1-|q_{\star,i}^Hq_{k,i}|)\\ &\le 2(1-|q_{\star,i}^Hq_{k,i}|^2)\\ &=2\|q_{k,i}q_{k,i}^H-q_{\star,i}q_{\star,i}^H\|_2^2. \end{aligned} \end{split}\]

Thus every aligned column has error bounded by a constant times \(\rho^k\). Collecting the phases in \(\Phi_k=\operatorname{diag}(\phi_{k,1},\ldots,\phi_{k,n})\) and bounding the matrix norm by the Frobenius norm gives

\[ \|Q_k\Phi_k^H-Q_\star\|_2\le C_Q\rho^k \]

for a constant \(C_Q\) independent of \(k\). Put \(V_k=Q_k\Phi_k^H\). Both \(V_k\) and \(Q_\star\) are unitary, so

\[\begin{split} \begin{aligned} \Phi_kT_k\Phi_k^H-T &=V_k^HAV_k-Q_\star^HAQ_\star\\ &=(V_k-Q_\star)^HAV_k +Q_\star^HA(V_k-Q_\star),\\ \|\Phi_kT_k\Phi_k^H-T\|_2 &\le 2\|A\|_2\,\|V_k-Q_\star\|_2\\ &\le 2\|A\|_2C_Q\rho^k. \end{aligned} \end{split}\]

Take \(C=2\|A\|_2C_Q\). Diagonal phase changes leave diagonal entries unchanged and preserve the magnitudes of other entries. Since \(T\) is upper triangular, the entrywise bounds follow.

The slowest adjacent eigenvalue ratio controls this overall bound. The block bounds above can give faster rates for particular parts of \(T_k\). We do not need to compute \(\Phi_k\) to read the diagonal approximations or monitor the entries below the diagonal; its role is to account for signs or phases when comparing the whole matrix with a fixed Schur factor. Entries above the diagonal may continue to change with those phases.

Convergence Rate of an Individual Eigenvalue#

A particular diagonal entry can converge faster than the whole matrix. The direction \(q_{k,j}\) is determined by the first \(j\) columns and their first \(j-1\) columns: it lies in the former space and is perpendicular to the latter. Its accuracy therefore depends on these two subspaces, whose rates are \(\rho_j\) and \(\rho_{j-1}\).

Corollary 1 (Individual Eigenvalue Error)

Under the assumptions of Theorem 29, for \(1<j<n\) there is a constant \(C_j\), independent of \(k\), such that

\[\begin{split} \boxed{ \begin{aligned} |(T_k)_{jj}-\lambda_j|&\le C_j\theta_j^k,\\ \theta_j&=\max\left\{ \left|\frac{\lambda_{j+1}}{\lambda_j}\right|, \left|\frac{\lambda_j}{\lambda_{j-1}}\right| \right\}. \end{aligned} } \end{split}\]

At the two ends, only one neighboring subspace contributes:

\[\begin{split} \begin{aligned} |(T_k)_{11}-\lambda_1| &\le C_1\left|\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\right|^k,\\ |(T_k)_{nn}-\lambda_n| &\le C_n\left|\frac{\lambda_n}{\lambda_{n-1}}\right|^k. \end{aligned} \end{split}\]

Proof. Use the phase-aligned unit vector \(v_{k,j}=q_{k,j}\overline{\phi_{k,j}}\) from the preceding proof. Its Rayleigh quotient is unchanged by this phase, so

\[ (T_k)_{jj}=v_{k,j}^HAv_{k,j}, \qquad \lambda_j=q_{\star,j}^HAq_{\star,j}. \]

Subtracting these expressions gives

\[\begin{split} \begin{aligned} (T_k)_{jj}-\lambda_j &=(v_{k,j}-q_{\star,j})^HAv_{k,j}\\ &\quad+q_{\star,j}^HA(v_{k,j}-q_{\star,j}),\\ |(T_k)_{jj}-\lambda_j| &\le 2\|A\|_2\,\|v_{k,j}-q_{\star,j}\|_2\\ &\le 2\sqrt{2}\|A\|_2(d_{k,j}+d_{k,j-1}). \end{aligned} \end{split}\]

For \(1<j<n\), substituting the two subspace bounds yields

\[\begin{split} \begin{aligned} |(T_k)_{jj}-\lambda_j| &\le 2\sqrt{2}\|A\|_2 (K_j\rho_j^k+K_{j-1}\rho_{j-1}^k)\\ &\le 2\sqrt{2}\|A\|_2(K_j+K_{j-1})\theta_j^k. \end{aligned} \end{split}\]

At \(j=1\) or \(j=n\), use \(d_{k,0}=d_{k,n}=0\) to obtain the endpoint bounds.

Thus a ratio near one elsewhere in the spectrum need not slow the approximation to \(\lambda_j\). Its bound is controlled by the two adjacent ratios. These are upper bounds; particular matrices or starting spaces can give faster convergence.

These are bounds for unshifted QR iteration under the stated assumptions. Equal eigenvalue magnitudes are outside this theorem; in real arithmetic, a nonreal conjugate pair must be treated together as a \(2\times2\) Schur block.

QR Iteration Steps#

This leads to the following simple algorithm, often called the “unshifted” QR iteration.

import numpy as np

# We use np.copy() so the original matrix A is not modified
Tk = np.copy(A)

# The 'not_converged' condition is abstract.
# In a real implementation, you would check if the
# sub-diagonal elements are all close to zero.
# For this example, we'll just run a fixed number of iterations.
num_iterations = 20

for k in range(num_iterations):
    # Step 1: Compute the QR decomposition of the current matrix
    # In the derivation's notation: T_k = U_{k+1} R_{k+1}
    # numpy.linalg.qr() returns Q, R (which are Uk, Rk here)
    Uk, Rk = np.linalg.qr(Tk)
    
    # Step 2: Compute the next matrix by reversing the factors
    # In the derivation's notation: T_{k+1} = R_{k+1} U_{k+1}
    # We store the new T_{k+1} back into the Tk variable
    # In Python, @ is the operator for matrix multiplication.
    Tk = Rk @ Uk

First Few Iterations#

Under the convergence assumptions above, the entries below the diagonal of \(T_k\) approach zero and its diagonal entries approach the eigenvalues. The first steps are:

  • Start: \(T_0 = A\)

  • Iteration 1:

    1. Compute QR decomposition: \(T_0 = U_1 R_1\)

    2. Compute next matrix: \(T_1 = R_1 U_1\)

  • Iteration 2:

    1. Compute QR decomposition: \(T_1 = U_2 R_2\)

    2. Compute next matrix: \(T_2 = R_2 U_2\)

  • Iteration 3:

    1. Compute QR decomposition: \(T_2 = U_3 R_3\)

    2. Compute next matrix: \(T_3 = R_3 U_3\)

  • … and so on.

Proof of Decomposition of \(A^k\)#

Proof. Use the product matrices \(\widetilde Q_k=U_1\cdots U_k\) and \(\widetilde R_k=R_k\cdots R_1\) defined in the theorem. We first relate \(T_k\) to \(A\), then use that relation to prove the formula for \(A^k\).

Each QR step gives the unitary similarity

\[ T_{j+1}=R_{j+1}U_{j+1} =U_{j+1}^HT_jU_{j+1}. \]

Starting from \(T_0=A\) and substituting repeatedly,

\[\begin{split} \begin{aligned} T_k &=U_k^H\cdots U_1^H\,A\,U_1\cdots U_k\\ &=\widetilde Q_k^HA\widetilde Q_k. \end{aligned} \end{split}\]

The second equality uses \((U_1\cdots U_k)^H=U_k^H\cdots U_1^H\). Since \(\widetilde Q_k\) is unitary, multiplying on the left by \(\widetilde Q_k\) gives

\[ A\widetilde Q_k=\widetilde Q_kT_k. \]

For \(k=1\), the first QR factorization gives \(A=U_1R_1=\widetilde Q_1\widetilde R_1\). Assume now that \(A^k=\widetilde Q_k\widetilde R_k\). Then

\[\begin{split} \begin{aligned} A^{k+1} &=A(\widetilde Q_k\widetilde R_k)\\ &=(A\widetilde Q_k)\widetilde R_k\\ &=\widetilde Q_kT_k\widetilde R_k\\ &=\widetilde Q_k(U_{k+1}R_{k+1})\widetilde R_k\\ &=(\widetilde Q_kU_{k+1})(R_{k+1}\widetilde R_k)\\ &=\widetilde Q_{k+1}\widetilde R_{k+1}. \end{aligned} \end{split}\]

This proves the result by induction. The new unitary factor is appended on the right of \(\widetilde Q_k\), while the new triangular factor is placed on the left of \(\widetilde R_k\). This explains the opposite orders of the two products.
